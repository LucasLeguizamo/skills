# Session 7 — Storytelling for Social Media (Sesión 7 — Storytelling para redes)

Problem: copied viral formats bring reach but no emotional bond, trust or sales → Answer: wrap every video in a compressed hero's journey where the *viewer* is the hero and you are the guide, anchored on a repeated origin story.

## Key ideas

- Storytelling is framed as the single most important skill on social today. Years ago just showing up was enough; now the accounts that win are the ones that tell stories well. Anything in your day can perform if it is told well.
- Stories do four things: (1) keep people watching (retention is what "hacks" the algorithm), (2) let a viewer step into your shoes so a concept can change their point of view, (3) build an emotional relationship with you/your brand (trust, attention, purchase), (4) shift perspectives, which is where persuasion lives.
- **Hero's journey = the one skeleton behind almost every story**: a main character has a problem they feel unable to solve alone → a guide appears and gives an insight/help → they set off → a path of ups and downs (failures and wins) → they solve the problem or avoid failure. The instructor walks through *Lord of the Rings*, *Star Wars* and *The Hunger Games* to show it is always the same structure.
- **Role inversion (core frame of the session):** in content, the protagonist is not you — "el personaje principal es el que está al otro lado" (the main character is the person on the other side of the screen). Your customer is the hero; you are the guide who hands over the insight. The common mistake is casting yourself as the protagonist.
- Two ways to put the viewer in the story: let them live *your* journey ("if they could, I can"), or narrate *their* problem so they recognize themselves.
- **The gap drives engagement**: the distance between the character's current situation and the problem they want to solve. The bigger the gap, the stronger the hook (e.g., "poor kid wants to defeat an empire"). Brands should tell transformations: where I/my client was → where we got → how.
- **Hook is where most of the effort goes.** If the viewer does not stop at the hook, nothing else exists. Viral formats are viral mainly because they already cracked the hook — often you only need to steal the hook and put your personality/story behind it.
- Viral list-style openers work because within ~1 second the viewer knows what the whole video is about *and* is curious for the rest (e.g., "don't say X, say Y" then the first item).
- The simpler and more real the pattern break, the better. When the feed saturated with fast cuts and constant stimuli, a creator broke the pattern by sitting still, filmed far away in dark horizontal framing, speaking slowly.
- **The problem can also be the hook** ("I got fired and here's how I found work"). Every video must contain a problem your customer identifies with — nobody knows your customer's pains better than you.
- **The path must show real ups and downs.** Content (especially on LinkedIn) lost closeness because everything looked perfect; the trend now is real life, good and bad.
- **Calculated vulnerability beats perfection**: "si uno ve una marca que es perfecta, que nunca falla, eso genera desconfianza" (a brand that never fails breeds distrust). But don't become "the brand of failures" — every failure must carry a learning.
- **The learning is the value.** Ask: what worked, what didn't, why did I fail, what would I do differently, what is the insight behind the story. Implicit learning is best; stating it at the end is acceptable while you improve. People now want lived experience, because AI already has all the theory.
- **Closeness beats guru status**: big referents teach from "I already did it", which can feel unreachable. You connect faster if the viewer sees you as similar to them (life stage, gender, profession, city, hobbies). Mine every trait from your "brand universe" (previous session) as a connection point.
- **Call to action, preferably implicit.** Options: an implicit close (the story itself implies the ask), a question to involve viewers, an invitation to give opinions, a strong closing line, an explicit offer (product/service), or a lead magnet ("comment WORD and I'll send you X"). CTAs/lead magnets are covered in more depth in a later session by Santiago Sánchez.
- **Origin story = the most important asset of a personal brand.** It explains *why you do what you do*; it is the only thing nobody can replicate ("your competitive advantage is being yourself"). Big personal brands (e.g., Gary Vaynerchuk working in his father's liquor store and "it's never too late", Alex Hormozi broke → gyms → sold a company → teaches business, Codie Sanchez buying "boring" businesses) repeat theirs relentlessly.
- Andrés Bilbao's origin element is simple and instantly recognizable: "co-founder of Rappi (and 30X)". It tells a new viewer in one line why to listen.
- Origin stories are rarely told in one piece; drip origin *elements* across many videos so they become associated with you. Do **not** put the credential line as the opening of every hook (videos start to feel identical); weave it into the story body instead ("when I was at company X…").
- Your origin doesn't need to be grand. Small defining moments work: quitting a job, changing careers, why you always wanted your profession, being at an early stage on the way to something. An "ordinary citizen" profile can itself become the differentiator against more decorated competitors.
- Social media accelerates reality but does not exceed it — you still need to be doing real things to tell.
- **Control your narrative**: if you don't choose how your story (including a dark or awkward part of your past) is told, others will choose it for you. A difficult element can be the thing that differentiates you from 99% of creators — frame it as fall → learning → comeback.
- **Virality is not the goal; conversion is.** A success-case video saved by 20 of the right people is worth more than a viral video reaching the wrong audience. Check whether a viral video served your objective; if not, move on. Niche accounts can sell more than viral ones.
- **Success stories** (yours or your clients') are the fastest validation. The instructor's own brand is built almost entirely on client cases, shown from start to finish of the engagement; posts about clients reliably bring leads. Also share mid-process wins (board meetings, speaking invitations), within what you are allowed to disclose.
- Give away small, high-value, experience-based tips (e.g., the clause in a contract you should never accept). When someone applies one and it works, they attribute results to you and come back when they need the paid service.
- **Selling inside stories** is the most powerful use: the viewer does not notice they are being sold to. Examples shown: a tutorial on camera transitions with the sponsor's product used throughout; a creator who builds a screenless voice-message gadget for a niece (and sells AI courses); Andrés Bilbao opening with a story about where the most successful young founders studied before pitching a free scholarship program; a car-for-sale ad embedded in an AI-photo tutorial; a daughter narrating her family's construction company with a family-drama arc.
- Simple, endlessly cloned story skits (e.g., the "Hey Leo… I have the best price… I need a guarantee" dialogue) can be adapted to any business as a low-effort start.
- B2B / niche reality: if your buyer is a narrow professional audience, either go deep-niche (talk about the specific problems only that buyer has) or go so massive that the buyer finds you anyway. Do not aim content at the end consumer if they are not your customer.
- Service sellers (agents, lawyers, accountants) sell themselves before the product; personal facts (e.g., choosing a flexible career to raise many children, decades in the industry) are the differentiator versus younger creators with better videos but less experience.
- Tell origin facts the way you'd tell a friend, with specifics ("I was at [specific university] when…"), not formal claims like "I studied at the most prestigious university" — specifics are what make it identifiable and believable.
- Rules of content are made to be broken: if your audience is asking to buy, test selling now rather than waiting to finish a volume target.

## Numbers & rules of thumb

- A campaign hero's-journey video, max 1 minute, reached ~2M organic views and had the highest saves+shares-to-views ratio of the account; it was AI-generated and took ~3 days of continuous work (only justified because the narrative was already validated many times).
- Text hooks: phrases of ~3 seconds shown on screen and spoken at the same time.
- A list/"don't say X" viral opener makes the viewer understand the whole video within ~1 second.
- "The 5 most viral hooks" stop being the most viral once everyone uses them.
- The over-stimulation trend described as "150 stimuli" per video → breaking it means going slow.
- A beginner adapting a copied story format got ~80k views, ~500 saves and ~150 comments on roughly their 3rd TikTok (vs. the ~1M-view original that inspired it).
- 20 saves from the right people > a viral video with the wrong audience.
- Reaching 5 right B2B buyers > reaching 1,000 wrong consumers; in 100 videos, at least one will reach those few buyers.
- First story will be bad; story #100 will be good — get to 100 fast.
- Origin story: never stop repeating it. You tire of it ~1,000× (even "a million times") faster than the audience; new viewers are the ones who need it. One repeated origin post seen on LinkedIn had ~17k likes.
- When adapting someone else's story format, change ~50–70% to make it yours (keep the hook/framework).
- If story videos slow your volume, do ~1 in 7 as a story video and keep the rest simple; but start now rather than after the volume target.
- The instructor has been creating for ~4 years.

## Frameworks / step-by-step methods

**Hero's journey (story skeleton)**
1. Main character (= your customer/viewer) with a problem they can't solve alone.
2. A guide (= you/your brand) gives an insight or help.
3. The character acts and goes through ups and downs.
4. They solve the problem or avoid failure; the transformation (gap closed) is shown.

**5-piece hero's journey for a single video**
1. **Hook** — most of your effort here.
2. **Problem** — creates the gap; must be a pain your customer recognizes (can double as the hook).
3. **Path of ups and downs** — real, imperfect, includes mistakes and decisions.
4. **Learning / value** — ideally implicit.
5. **Call to action** — preferably implicit; or question, opinion prompt, strong closing line, offer, or lead magnet.

**Four levers to build a hook**
1. **Interrupt the pattern** — do what the feed doesn't (e.g., still, slow, dark, horizontal).
2. **Different point of view** — say what nobody says; must be your genuine view, not invented controversy (e.g., Andrés Bilbao's "3 businesses I wouldn't start", opening with the one everyone thinks is safe, plus his credential).
3. **Challenge an industry belief** — insiders hold contrarian beliefs earned by experience (e.g., "theory doesn't matter, making videos does"; "virality doesn't matter").
4. **Generate curiosity** — unexpected juxtapositions (e.g., "We were the #1 business podcast and our membership died in 3 months"; "I beat the Vice President on the most boring social network").

**Story types to test (each brand should try all and keep what resonates)**
1. **Origin story** (most important): why you do what you do.
2. **Error / failure / frustration**: past or current; always paired with a learning (e.g., closing a store and why; frustration at being copied while showing your product).
3. **Success**: your own or your clients' (most validating; highest lead yield).
4. **Sales story**: the sale camouflaged inside a story or tutorial.

**Finding and testing your origin story**
1. List 2–3 defining elements of your life/company (where you studied, where you worked and why you left, why you chose this field, unusual life facts).
2. Look for a common thread linking them (e.g., "university didn't teach what I do now" + "left a well-known company to start my own").
3. Tell each element on camera in separate videos; publish.
4. Read TikTok retention graphs to see where viewers drop (e.g., a name the audience doesn't know may cause a drop).
5. Keep what connects (topics like parenthood, alma mater, past employer reveal which elements resonate), refine wording each time, and repeat forever.
6. Advice frame: imagine giving advice to yourself 10 years ago — that person (if they resemble your customer) is your audience.

**Progression from copying to storytelling**
1. Start with the simplest formats to copy (swap names/details) while editing skills grow.
2. Then pick a story video from your industry you like and adapt it to your real elements (keep the hook; change 50–70%).
3. Gradually add more of your own storytelling on top of copied formats; long-term goal is to deconstruct any viral format and build your own story on it.

## Pitfalls / what instructors warned against

- Casting yourself as the protagonist instead of the viewer.
- Perfect, flawless brand stories — they generate distrust.
- Being "the brand of failures": failure without a learning.
- Chasing virality for its own sake; keeping a viral direction that doesn't serve your goal.
- Copying the "most viral hooks" everyone already uses — they've stopped working; look for what still surprises you.
- Opening every video with the same credential line — videos start to look identical; weave it into the story.
- Stopping repetition of your origin story because *you* are bored of it.
- Inventing stories or fake contrarian takes — the instructor would never fabricate; it must be real.
- Vague, formal claims ("the most prestigious university") instead of concrete, conversational specifics.
- Hiding a difficult past and letting others own the narrative.
- Outsourcing editing too early — the editor can become your main bottleneck; build speed in-house first.
- Relying only on copied formats: they bring reach but erode the skill of thinking up and structuring a story (compared to how ChatGPT can erode skills).
- Using podcast clipping as a substitute for learning to talk to the camera alone; it's better than nothing, but avoiding the hard format delays the core skill.
- Expecting a long, polished story production per video — an elaborate origin film is weeks of team work; day to day, adapt existing formats.
- Getting frustrated or blaming the method when the first story underperforms.
- Talking to end consumers when your buyer is a niche B2B professional.

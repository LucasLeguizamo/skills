# Session 2 — The Infinite Game: A System to Never Stop Creating (Sesión 2 — El juego infinito: un sistema para nunca dejar de crear)

Problem: most people quit social content before it pays off → Answer: build a personal system (routine + enjoyment + audience connection) that makes publishing daily the default, and push to 100 videos fast.

## Key ideas

- **Social networks are now entertainment platforms.** TikTok (2016) swapped the "social graph" for interest-based discovery; every other platform copied it. They compete with Netflix in short form, so **they need creators** and reward anyone who retains viewers. That is the opportunity: equal conditions for everyone.
- **The whole framework is one line:** publish, be consistent, raise volume, learn, iterate. Theory matters far less than having a system that never stops publishing. The only thing that decides success is whether you publish.
- **The rules changed, the basics did not:** constancy, fast learning, iteration, authenticity ("be yourself, don't invent"). Results are not immediate; that delay is what filters out most people.
- **The barrier is mental, not technical.** Creator skills are learned by doing; what you see on feeds is the output of many bad early videos. Every frustration phase changes shape but never ends; early on it usually means "not enough iterations yet."
- **Compete only against yourself last week.** Never benchmark against other creators.
- **Speed = time from idea to published.** The unit of progress is the published video, not the idea or the script.
- **Bad early videos cost nothing:** with a recommendation algorithm, "failing" means nobody saw it — "nadie vio tu fracaso" (nobody saw your failure). The real early pain is friends watching; mockery usually reflects their own insecurity.
- **Perfectionism is enemy #1.** If you can't fix it in time, publish anyway. Established brands over-polish; early-stage businesses can't afford to.
- **Identity is found by publishing, not by thinking.** It won't show up at video 7 (you'll feel bad); it emerges around video 100. Questions at video 100: what did I like, what went viral and why, what isn't me, what doesn't serve my goal.
- **Copying formats is not cheating; it's the fast lane.** Content lives between "copy what works" and "who I am." Too many variables (hook, script, copy, edit, message) to innovate on all at once; a viral video already solved that combination. Start by copying "a lo feo" (plainly), add your style later.
- **Hygiene rule:** never say anything you don't agree with, even if it would go viral, so you can stand behind it when it spreads.
- **Only three ways to create value: entertain, teach, inspire.** You don't need all three; pick one or two. Entertaining is the hardest. Teaching + inspiring with a pinch of entertainment is the easiest path. Don't chase trends/dances you don't enjoy.
- **Organic rewards value, not production quality.** Many top creators just talk straight to camera. During the first 100 videos: **don't sell**, give value.
- **Define your "why" before the account.** The network is a tool, not the end. The size of the why determines how much discomfort you'll tolerate. A selfish why is fine; having none makes the infinite game nearly impossible.
- **Being an influencer is the worst business:** you are the product, a platform change can kill it, and you can never stop. Content should leverage a business (it can pause; the company keeps running).
- **Content must point at the goal:** awareness → reach; sales → a funnel (content → website/offer).
- **Personal brand first** (instructor's current bet): lower risk, survives business failure, highest current opportunity. Company accounts work on Instagram/TikTok too; easiest version is a person as the face of the company. Avatars/AI presenters are possible but "complicating yourself."
- **Large orgs move slowly:** create a small autonomous team and put a decision-maker (owner/manager) in the loop so no one blocks experiments. Solo founders must give themselves that speed and prioritize content.
- **Don't advertise your tool/product in early videos.** Nobody watches a software demo mid-scroll. First learn to capture your customer's attention with what interests them; weave the product in after ~video 100.
- **Build a "universe," not a product catalogue.** A single dish/product runs out of topics fast; expand to the industry, the city/region, the life of running the business, characters/sketches. Indirect content (e.g., a store whose owners do comedy sketches, never filming products) can outperform product content.
- **B2B/distributor niches:** make content that helps your buyer do their job better (e.g., sales strategies for distributors); they'll learn you exist. If targeting end consumers would attract wrong leads, add an automated qualifying filter.
- **Separate unrelated brands.** Don't cross-post between unrelated niches; separate accounts and looks are fine even with the same face.
- **Followers are not the metric.** Track **average views and interactions/engagement**; followers are only a secondary signal. Old big accounts can have dead reach; small accounts with strong content can out-reach them.
- **Organic vs paid:** platform ad AIs make targeting less important and the creative more important, so organic skills compound into paid.
- **Learning account:** a separate profile that only follows/likes/saves niche references, trained to become a format search engine.

## Numbers & rules of thumb

- TikTok launched 2016; fastest network to reach ~1B active users.
- Cadence: **minimum 1 video per day**; that is the single goal.
- **100 videos ≈ 3 months** at 1/day; **100 posts ≈ 80% of the path**; aim to "have it figured out" in **~12 weeks**.
- 1 video/week = **100 weeks** to learn the same; people at 1/week quit around 50 weeks with only ~50 videos.
- Weekly challenge: **7 videos in 7 days** (one daily, start today).
- Cohort reality: of **30–40** students, only **~5** take daily publishing seriously.
- Paid ads: if not running them, **turn them off / don't start for 3 months** and learn organic; if already running, keep them and run organic in parallel, judged separately (paid = CAC; organic is harder to measure).
- Instructor's content is **~99% pure value**, no selling, no outbound DMs.
- Hiring someone to film + edit removes **~50%** of the problem (if you can afford it).
- Expected timeline: **1 month = nothing yet; ~3 months = clearer; ~6 months = more; ~1 year = flying.** Growth can be fast; positioning takes time.
- Within 100 well-copied videos, a strong personal brand could plausibly reach **~50k–100k followers**; 100 at 0 views shouldn't happen if you follow the method.
- Even with ~1M TikTok followers, a bad video can get ~0 views.
- Sweet-spot length: **~40 s**; also test **~20 s** and **~2 min** formats you like.
- A ~30 s video ≈ **~10 short phrases** recorded one at a time and stitched.
- Max **3 content pillars** per personal account.
- Under **~3,000 followers** with a confused/mixed history: just start a fresh account.
- After consistent publishing, **~15 videos with poor results** = something is wrong; diagnose and double down on what works.
- Restaurant/product-only universe runs out in **~20 posts**.
- Clipping long content: ~**15 clips from 2 h** of manual work (AI tools do it in minutes); typically only **~2 of 15** clips work; clips **do not count** toward the 100; on TikTok you can push up to ~**50** clips.
- If mixing carousels and videos in the challenge: do **~200 pieces** (keep the 100 of each type).
- Carousels: shorter mixed carousels (~5 slides) were observed to beat long ~20-slide ones (participant observation the instructor accepted).
- Instructor track record (context for "anyone can learn"): first account in ~1 year → ~150k TikTok / ~50k Instagram; second account in ~1–1.5 years → ~950k TikTok, ~450k Instagram, ~110k YouTube, plus Facebook; ~1.6M total followers created with only a phone.
- A political campaign run on these principles went from ~1% voting intention to ~1.2M votes in a primary in ~4 months.

## Frameworks / step-by-step methods

**The infinite-game triangle (in this order):**
1. **Fit content into your routine** — don't adapt your life to content; insert content where you already live (home, office, commute). Kill ideas that need locations you're rarely at.
2. **Find something you enjoy** in the process (editing, the topic, the people/connections). Keep that part for yourself even if you delegate others. Give yourself time; at first everything feels awful.
3. **Connect with your audience** — the end result of 1 and 2 plus iteration.
With all three in place, stopping stops making sense. Optimize everything to reduce friction and raise speed.

**Solve friction concretely:** (a) how/where you record, (b) who edits. Choose a batching style that fits you (block one afternoon a week vs. record spontaneously each day).

**The 100-video system:**
1. Commit to one video per day (minimum), starting now, not "Monday."
2. Copy proven formats from your niche (and other languages); change little at first.
3. Publish without perfecting; measure with "what did I learn?", not views.
4. Notice what feels like "me" vs "not me"; adjust.
5. At ~100, decide identity, strategy, platform emphasis, and whether to hire.
Already creating consistently? Use the same system to test formats and break plateaus.

**Copy ↔ identity spectrum:** pure clone (valid for some goals) ← middle (recommended: what works + what I like) → fully original (longer, harder). Your "why" decides where you sit.

**Distribution routine:** post to TikTok first → Instagram Reels (toggle auto-share to Facebook) → test YouTube Shorts. Same video everywhere during the 100; compare platforms afterwards. Put all video effort into Reels; Stories are for community, engagement and behind-the-scenes, not growth.

**Instagram trial reels:** re-run your **best** performers (not the weakest) to non-followers; vary the hook/copy in ~3 versions and learn which wins.

**Learning account setup:** create a separate account; follow, like and save only niche references; use its feed and the TikTok search (preferred) as your format finder. Do it manually first before automating trend research.

**Recording without teleprompter:** read one short phrase, say it to camera, repeat; cut out the gaps in editing.

**Repurposing:** recycle content that worked (new format, new hook, LinkedIn post → screenshot/carousel/to-camera). If it didn't work, make new content instead.

## Tools mentioned

- **TikTok search** — preferred search engine for formats; Instagram search also works.
- **CapCut** — editing (remove its end watermark).
- **Instagram trial reels** — test reels shown to non-followers.
- **Instagram → Facebook auto-share toggle**; **YouTube Shorts** as extra outlet.
- **ManyChat** (or similar automation) — filter/qualify inbound leads.
- **AI clipping tool (e.g., Opus Clip)** — fast clipping; low hit rate.
- **Teleprompter** — optional; buy only if it truly makes it easier.
- **Microphone** — optional; DJI wireless mic named as the best (and most expensive); search "top 5 microphones for content" before buying. No prices given. Default advice: buy nothing, use your phone.
- **LinkedIn** — text-only option when recording video isn't feasible (B2B).

## Pitfalls / what instructors warned against

- Waiting to start ("I'll start Monday"); planning instead of publishing — no 6-hour strategy replaces 200 videos.
- Publishing once a week; expecting results in a month or six weeks.
- Perfectionism and over-polished production.
- Selling or demoing your product in the first ~100 videos.
- Using followers as the main KPI; comparing yourself to other creators.
- Content ideas that are hard to execute (filming at client sites, etc.).
- Chasing trends/dances you don't like; forcing entertainment if it isn't you.
- Saying things you disagree with for virality.
- Becoming an influencer with no business behind it.
- Counting AI/manual clips of long content toward the learning 100; complex clipping/volume systems before the basics.
- Mixing unrelated niches on one account; feeding a confused tiny account instead of restarting.
- Re-boosting your weakest videos with trial reels instead of your winners.
- Buying gear before you know what you need.
- Inventing everything from scratch at the start (too many variables, high failure rate).
